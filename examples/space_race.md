## The Beep {mood=tension}

[[visual: Sputnik 1 satellite]]
On the night of October 4, 1957, radio operators around the world picked up a strange sound from the sky. A steady beep, beep, beep, repeating every few seconds, coming from something that had never existed before: a machine in orbit around the Earth.

[[visual: Sputnik 1 replica antennas]]
It was a polished metal sphere, fifty-eight centimeters across, with four long antennas trailing behind it. It weighed just eighty-three kilograms. And it belonged to the Soviet Union.

[[visual: R-7 rocket launch Baikonur]]
The rocket that carried it into space was the R-7, the world's first intercontinental ballistic missile. The same machine that could lift a satellite into orbit could, in principle, drop a nuclear warhead anywhere on Earth. Everyone understood what that meant.

[[visual: Cold War rocket launch 1950s]]
In the United States, the beep landed like a punch. If the Soviets could put a satellite over America, what else could they put up there? Twelve years later, two men would stand on the Moon. This is the story of how that happened.

## The Shock {mood=tension}

[[visual: Sputnik 1 satellite model]]
[[place: Baikonur Cosmodrome · Kazakhstan, 1957]]
Sputnik One circled the planet once every ninety-six minutes. It was moving at about eight kilometers per second, eighteen thousand miles an hour. It sent its radio signal back to Earth for three weeks, until its batteries ran dry, and then it kept circling in silence until January 1958, when it fell back into the atmosphere.

[[visual: radio operators listening Sputnik signal]]
Anyone with a decent shortwave radio could tune in and hear it. Amateur radio operators recorded the signal, newspapers printed its schedule, and people stepped outside at dusk to look for the glint of the rocket body crossing the sky. Space was no longer a dream. It was overhead, and it was not American.

[[visual: Laika dog Sputnik 2]]
The Soviets did not wait. Less than a month later, Sputnik Two carried a passenger into orbit: a small dog from the streets of Moscow named Laika. She became the first animal to orbit the Earth. She did not survive the flight.

[[visual: Vanguard rocket explosion 1957]]
[[place: Cape Canaveral, Florida · December 1957]]
America answered with Vanguard. In front of the world's press, the rocket rose about four feet off the pad, lost thrust, and fell back in a ball of fire. Newspapers had a field day. They called it Flopnik, Kaputnik, and Stayputnik.

[[visual: Vanguard rocket on launch pad]]
The failure was reported around the world. In the middle of the Cold War, it looked like proof that America was falling behind, not just in rockets, but in science, education and industry.

[[visual: Explorer 1 launch 1958]]
Two months later, a team that included the rocket engineer Wernher von Braun finally put America's first satellite, Explorer One, into orbit. It made the first great discovery of the space age: belts of radiation surrounding the Earth, now named after the scientist who found them, James Van Allen. Explorer One kept sending data for nearly four months, until its batteries were exhausted.

[[timeline: The first months | Oct 1957: Sputnik 1; Nov 1957: Sputnik 2; Jan 1958: Explorer 1; 1958: NASA founded]]
That same year, the United States created a new civilian agency to run the race. It was called the National Aeronautics and Space Administration. NASA.

[[visual: Luna 3 far side of the Moon photograph]]
Meanwhile, Soviet robots were already heading for the Moon itself. In 1959, Luna Two became the first spacecraft to strike the lunar surface. Weeks later, Luna Three sent back the first photographs of the Moon's far side, a face of the Moon no human had ever seen.

## The First Man {mood=wonder}

[[visual: Yuri Gagarin portrait]]
On April 12, 1961, a twenty-seven-year-old Soviet pilot named Yuri Gagarin climbed into a capsule called Vostok One. As the engines roared, he shouted a single word over the radio: Poyekhali. Let's go.

[[visual: Vostok 1 capsule]]
Gagarin circled the Earth once. The entire flight, from launch to landing, took one hundred and eight minutes. Near the end, he ejected from the capsule and came down under his own parachute in a field in the Soviet Union.

[[visual: Vostok rocket launch]]
At its highest point, Vostok One was more than three hundred kilometers above the Earth, moving at orbital speed. Gagarin had been a passenger in a capsule that was largely controlled from the ground, but none of that mattered. A human being had seen the whole curve of the planet.

[[visual: Yuri Gagarin Moscow celebration]]
He came home a hero, and the Soviet Union came home with another first. Every major milestone of the space age so far had a Soviet flag on it.

[[visual: Alan Shepard Freedom 7]]
Three weeks later, America launched Alan Shepard aboard Freedom Seven. His flight lasted fifteen minutes and twenty-two seconds. He went up, and he came down in the Atlantic. He did not reach orbit. But America was in the game.

[[visual: Freedom 7 recovery helicopter]]
Shepard's capsule climbed more than one hundred miles high and landed about three hundred miles downrange. Within minutes, a helicopter lifted him from the ocean onto the deck of a waiting aircraft carrier. The flight was short, but it was watched live across the country.

[[compare: First humans in space | Gagarin: 108 min; Shepard: 15 min]]
Side by side, the gap was obvious. One nation had circled the planet. The other had made a brave, short hop. The new American president decided that was not good enough.

## The Challenge {mood=driving}

[[visual: John F. Kennedy speech Congress 1961]]
[[place: Washington, D.C. · May 25, 1961]]
On May 25, 1961, President John F. Kennedy stood before Congress and asked for something that, at the time, nobody knew how to do.

[[quote: This nation should commit itself to achieving the goal, before this decade is out, of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to the Earth. | John F. Kennedy, May 25, 1961]]
He wanted Americans on the Moon, and back, within nine years. When he said it, the United States had a grand total of fifteen minutes of human spaceflight experience.

[[visual: Kennedy Rice University speech]]
[[place: Rice University · Houston, Texas]]
More than a year later, in a football stadium in Houston, he explained why. We choose to go to the Moon, he said, not because it is easy, but because it is hard.

[[visual: Manned Spacecraft Center Houston 1960s]]
The challenge reshaped a nation. Houston became the home of a new center for human spaceflight. Florida's coast turned into a launch complex. Engineers, factories and universities across the country were pulled into a single project.

[[visual: Wernher von Braun Saturn rocket]]
The plan they chose was called lunar orbit rendezvous. Instead of landing one huge spacecraft on the Moon, Apollo would send a mothership into lunar orbit and drop a small, separate lander to the surface. It was risky, but it was the only way to make the numbers work in time.

[[number: 400,000 | people working on Apollo at its peak]]
At its peak, the Apollo program employed four hundred thousand people and drew on more than twenty thousand companies and universities.

## Learning to Fly {mood=driving}

[[visual: John Glenn Friendship 7]]
First, America had to learn to stay up there. On February 20, 1962, John Glenn rode Friendship Seven into orbit and circled the Earth three times. The country finally had its own orbital hero.

[[visual: Valentina Tereshkova]]
The Soviets kept adding firsts. In June 1963, Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space. Flying alone aboard Vostok Six, she orbited the Earth forty-eight times and spent almost three days in space. She is still the only woman ever to have flown a solo space mission.

[[visual: Alexei Leonov spacewalk Voskhod 2]]
In March 1965, Alexei Leonov opened a hatch and floated out into the void, the first human to walk in space. He stayed outside for about twelve minutes. His suit ballooned so badly in the vacuum that he struggled to squeeze back inside.

[[visual: Ed White spacewalk Gemini 4]]
Less than three months later, Ed White became the first American to walk in space during the flight of Gemini Four. He and Jim McDivitt circled the Earth sixty-six times in four days. He floated at the end of a tether, steering himself with a small handheld gas gun, and later said that coming back inside was the saddest moment of his life.

[[visual: Gemini 7 seen from Gemini 6 rendezvous]]
America's answer was Project Gemini, a two-person spacecraft built to practice everything a Moon landing would need. Long flights. Spacewalks. And the hardest trick of all: finding another spacecraft in orbit and meeting it.

[[visual: Gemini 8 Agena docking]]
In December 1965, Gemini Six and Gemini Seven flew within a few meters of each other.

[[visual: Gemini 7 Borman Lovell]]
Gemini Seven was also an endurance test. Frank Borman and Jim Lovell spent nearly fourteen days in orbit, circling the Earth two hundred and six times inside a cabin about the size of the front seat of a small car. It proved that people could survive long enough to reach the Moon and return. In March 1966, a quiet test pilot named Neil Armstrong performed the first docking of two spacecraft in orbit.

[[list: What Gemini proved | Long flights in orbit; Working outside the spacecraft; Rendezvous; Docking]]
By the end of Gemini, the United States had done all the things it needed for a lunar mission. What it did not yet have was a rocket big enough to get there.

## The Machine {mood=wonder}

[[visual: Saturn V rocket Vehicle Assembly Building]]
That rocket was the Saturn Five. Stacked with the Apollo spacecraft on top, it stood three hundred and sixty-three feet tall, taller than the Statue of Liberty. At its base, it was thirty-three feet wide.

[[visual: Saturn V F-1 engines]]
Its first stage was powered by five F-1 engines. They remain the most powerful single-chamber liquid-fuelled rocket engines ever built.

[[number: 363 | feet tall, the Saturn V]]
Everything about the Saturn Five was enormous: the factories that built it, the barges that carried its stages, and the building that assembled it, one of the largest structures on Earth by volume.

[[visual: Apollo command module spacecraft]]
On top of it rode the Apollo spacecraft. A cone-shaped command module for three astronauts, a service module with the engine and supplies, and a spindly lunar module designed to land on the Moon and fly back up.

[[visual: Apollo lunar module test]]
Nothing like it had ever been flown. Every part had to work, every time, two hundred thousand miles from the nearest repair shop.

[[visual: Saturn V first stage S-IC]]
The work was spread across the whole country. Boeing built the first stage. Other contractors built the upper stages, the engines, the guidance computers and the spacecraft. Each stage had to be shipped to Florida and stacked inside the assembly building before it could be rolled to the pad.

## Fire {mood=somber}

[[visual: Apollo 1 crew Grissom White Chaffee]]
On January 27, 1967, three astronauts sat strapped inside the first Apollo command module on the launch pad. They were rehearsing the countdown at Launch Complex Thirty-Four in Florida. It was a routine test. The rocket beneath them was not even fuelled.

[[visual: Apollo 1 command module fire damage]]
An electrical spark ignited the cabin. Inside a capsule filled with pure oxygen, the fire spread in seconds. The hatch opened inward and could not be forced open against the pressure. Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee were killed.

[[visual: Apollo 1 investigation]]
NASA stopped. No American would fly in space again until October 1968. The spacecraft was redesigned from the inside out, with a hatch that opened outward in seconds, fireproof materials, and a safer cabin atmosphere on the pad.

[[visual: Soyuz spacecraft 1960s]]
The Soviet program was suffering too. Its chief designer, Sergei Korolev, the man behind Sputnik and Gagarin, had died in January 1966. In April 1967, cosmonaut Vladimir Komarov was killed when the parachute of Soyuz One failed.

[[visual: astronaut memorial 1967]]
Both sides had learned the same lesson. The race was not only against each other. It was against physics, and physics did not forgive mistakes.

[[visual: Apollo 7 crew in orbit]]
In October 1968, Apollo Seven finally carried a crew into orbit: Wally Schirra, Donn Eisele and Walter Cunningham. For eleven days they tested the redesigned spacecraft. It worked. Apollo was flying again.

## Around the Moon {mood=wonder}

[[visual: Apollo 8 launch Saturn V]]
[[place: Kennedy Space Center · December 21, 1968]]
On December 21, 1968, a Saturn Five lifted off carrying Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders. Their mission, Apollo Eight, was bolder than anything that had come before. They were going to the Moon.

[[visual: Apollo 8 Earth from space]]
It was only the second crewed Apollo flight, and the first time people would ride the Saturn Five. As the spacecraft coasted away, the crew became the first humans to see the whole Earth as a single round world, shrinking behind them.

[[visual: Apollo 8 Moon surface from orbit]]
Three days later, they slipped behind the Moon and out of radio contact with Earth. No humans had ever seen its far side with their own eyes. Apollo Eight orbited the Moon ten times.

[[visual: Earthrise Apollo 8]]
On one of those orbits, the Earth rose over the lunar horizon: a small blue and white ball, floating alone in the black. Bill Anders grabbed a camera. The photograph became known as Earthrise.

[[visual: Apollo 8 crew Christmas Eve broadcast]]
On Christmas Eve, the crew read the opening lines of the Book of Genesis to an audience on Earth. Then they fired their engine and headed home.

[[number: 10 | orbits of the Moon by Apollo 8]]
After ten orbits, Apollo Eight returned safely. The Moon was now within reach. Only the landing was left.

[[visual: Apollo 9 lunar module Spider]]
Two more rehearsals followed. In March 1969, Apollo Nine tested the lunar module in Earth orbit, flying it away from the command module and docking again. In May, Apollo Ten flew the whole mission to the Moon except the final descent, bringing its lander within about fifteen kilometers of the surface.

## The Eagle Has Landed {mood=tension}

[[visual: Apollo 11 crew Armstrong Collins Aldrin]]
On July 16, 1969, Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins launched aboard Apollo Eleven. Four days later, Armstrong and Aldrin climbed into the lunar module Eagle and separated from Collins in the command module Columbia.

[[visual: Apollo 11 launch crowd spectators]]
An estimated one million people lined the highways and beaches near the launch site to watch the Saturn Five climb into the sky. In the United States alone, about twenty-five million people watched the launch on television.

[[visual: Apollo 11 lunar module Eagle descent]]
As Eagle descended, alarms began to flash. Program alarm twelve-oh-two. Then twelve-oh-one. The guidance computer was overloaded. In Houston, a young engineer studied the codes and made the call. Keep going.

[[visual: Apollo 11 Mission Control]]
[[place: Mission Control · Houston, Texas]]
Then Armstrong saw that the computer was steering them into a field of boulders. He took manual control and flew Eagle over the rocks, searching for a clear spot, while the fuel gauge kept falling.

[[visual: Apollo 11 Mission Control Houston consoles]]
In Mission Control, nobody spoke. Flight controllers watched the numbers, knowing that if the fuel ran too low the crew would have to abort and fly back to orbit without ever touching the surface.

[[visual: Apollo 11 lunar module on the Moon]]
On July 20, 1969, Eagle touched down in the Sea of Tranquility. Michael Collins was alone in lunar orbit aboard Columbia, waiting for them to come back. Armstrong's voice came over the radio: Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.

## One Small Step {mood=uplifting}

[[visual: Neil Armstrong first step Moon]]
Hours later, Armstrong climbed down the ladder and stepped onto the surface. It was about six hours after the landing. A black-and-white television camera on the side of the lander sent the picture back to Earth.

[[quote: That's one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind. | Neil Armstrong, July 1969]]
Aldrin followed him down. For the next couple of hours, the two men walked on another world, planted a flag, set up experiments and spoke by radio with the President in the White House.

[[visual: Buzz Aldrin on the Moon]]
They collected twenty-one and a half kilograms of lunar rock and soil. After more than twenty-one hours on the surface, they lifted off and rejoined Collins in lunar orbit.

[[visual: Apollo 11 plaque lunar module]]
They left behind a flag, scientific instruments and a small plaque on the leg of the lander. It reads: Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969, A.D. We came in peace for all mankind.

[[visual: Apollo 11 splashdown recovery]]
[[place: Pacific Ocean · July 24, 1969]]
On July 24, 1969, Columbia splashed down in the Pacific Ocean. Kennedy's deadline had been met with five months to spare.

[[visual: Apollo 11 mobile quarantine facility]]
Because no one could be completely sure the Moon was sterile, the three astronauts walked straight from the helicopter into a sealed trailer. They began twenty-one days of quarantine before they could hug their families.

[[timeline: Twelve years | 1957: Sputnik; 1961: Gagarin; 1965: First spacewalk; 1968: Apollo 8; 1969: Apollo 11]]
From a beeping sphere to human footprints on the Moon had taken less than twelve years.

## After the Race {mood=uplifting}

[[visual: Soviet N1 rocket]]
The Soviet Union had been building its own Moon rocket, the giant N1. All four of its launch attempts, between 1969 and 1972, ended in failure. No cosmonaut ever went to the Moon.

[[visual: Luna 16 sample return]]
Soviet robots kept exploring instead. In 1966, Luna Nine had made the first soft landing on the Moon and sent back pictures from its surface. In 1970, Luna Sixteen scooped up lunar soil and returned it to Earth, the first sample ever brought back by a robot.

[[visual: Apollo 17 astronaut lunar rover]]
America went back again and again. Six Apollo missions landed on the Moon, the last, Apollo Seventeen, in December 1972. In those six flights, twelve people walked on its surface.

[[number: $25.4 billion | the cost of the Apollo program]]
Apollo cost twenty-five point four billion dollars at the time, one of the largest investments ever made by any nation in peacetime. In today's money, that is well over two hundred and fifty billion dollars.

[[visual: Apollo Soyuz handshake 1975]]
And in 1975, an American Apollo spacecraft and a Soviet Soyuz met in orbit. Their crews opened the hatches and shook hands. The race that began with a beep ended with a handshake.

[[visual: Earth from space Apollo]]
The Space Race was born of fear and rivalry. What it left behind was a photograph of a small blue planet, and the knowledge that humans can leave it. If you enjoyed this story, subscribe for more documentaries like it.
