## Fifty-Six Hours {mood=tension}

[[visual: Apollo 13 service module damage]]
Fifty-six hours into the flight to the Moon, three astronauts heard a bang. Warning lights flooded the panel, and oxygen was venting into space.

[[quote: Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here. | Jack Swigert, April 13, 1970]]
Twenty-six seconds later, Jack Swigert radioed the most famous understatement in the history of spaceflight.

## A Routine Flight {mood=neutral}

[[visual: Apollo 13 launch Saturn V]]
[[place: Kennedy Space Center · April 11, 1970]]
Apollo 13 lifted off on April 11, 1970. It was meant to be the third landing on the Moon, and by then, the public had almost stopped watching.

[[visual: Apollo 13 prime crew portrait]]
Commander Jim Lovell flew with Fred Haise and Jack Swigert. Swigert was a late replacement for Ken Mattingly, who was grounded days before launch after exposure to rubella.

## The Explosion {mood=tension}

[[visual: Apollo 13 oxygen tank]]
During a routine stir of oxygen tank number two, damaged wiring inside the tank sparked. The fire blew out the side of the service module.

[[visual: Mission Control Apollo 13]]
[[place: Mission Control · Houston, Texas]]
In Houston, flight controllers watched the numbers fall. Two of the three fuel cells were dead, and the landing was over. The only question left was whether anyone could get home.

## The Lifeboat {mood=somber}

[[visual: Apollo 13 lunar module Aquarius]]
The answer was docked to the nose of the ship: the lunar module, Aquarius. The crew powered down their command module and moved into the lander.

[[compare: Built for vs needed | Built for: 45 hours; Needed: 87 hours]]
But Aquarius was built to keep two astronauts alive for about 45 hours on the Moon. Now it had to carry three men for nearly four days.

[[visual: Apollo 13 crew inside lunar module]]
To save power, almost everything was switched off. Inside the darkened spacecraft, the temperature fell to just three degrees Celsius.

## A Square Filter in a Round Hole {mood=driving}

[[visual: Apollo 13 lithium hydroxide canister mailbox]]
Then came a quieter threat. The carbon dioxide filters in the lander were round, and the spare filters from the command module were square.

[[list: The mailbox, built from | Plastic covers from manuals; Duct tape; Whatever was on board]]
Engineers on the ground built a fix from what the crew had on board: plastic covers ripped from procedure manuals, duct tape, and whatever else they could find. They called it the mailbox, and it worked.

## Around the Moon {mood=wonder}

[[visual: Apollo 13 far side of the Moon]]
There was no way to turn around. Apollo 13 had to swing behind the Moon and use its gravity to sling the crew back toward Earth.

[[number: 248,655 | miles from Earth | anchor=two]]
On April 14, the crew was two hundred forty-eight thousand, six hundred fifty-five miles from Earth. No human had ever been that far from home, and the record stood for more than half a century.

## Coming Home {mood=uplifting}

[[visual: Apollo 13 service module jettison]]
Near Earth, the crew cast off the crippled service module and finally saw the damage. An entire panel was gone.

[[visual: Apollo 13 reentry Odyssey]]
Reentry blackout usually lasted four minutes. This time it lasted six. Mission Control waited in silence, until Swigert's voice came back over the radio.

[[timeline: The mission | April 11: Launch; April 13: Tank explodes; April 14: Around the Moon; April 17: Splashdown]]
[[place: South Pacific Ocean · April 17, 1970]]
On April 17, Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific, a few miles from the recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima. All three men were alive.

[[visual: Apollo 13 crew recovery USS Iwo Jima]]
